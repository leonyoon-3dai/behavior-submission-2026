\begin{abstract}
We study whether small learned action corrections can improve a frozen household-manipulation policy. Our system retains Ilia's released BEHAVIOR policy and adds task-specific EXPO-RT auxiliary networks for candidate editing and selection. In a paired development panel of six tasks and 36 rollouts, mean final goal progress decreases from 0.455 to 0.329, with full successes decreasing from 5/18 to 1/18. A separate, frozen-policy challenge panel completes 12 rollouts across three tasks, achieving mean final goal progress 0.224206 and no full successes. We present the implementation, original visual evidence, and reproducibility record. The evidence documents a completed submission and a negative aggregate result, rather than a general improvement claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Study question and contribution}
Household tasks require sequences of navigation, grasping, transport, and placement. A plausible action at one moment may still fail to complete the overall task. We test a modest intervention: preserve an existing manipulation policy, then learn small corrections and select among its proposed actions.

This report contributes a transparent \textbf{implementation and evaluation record}: a paired pilot, a separate partial challenge submission, and a visual failure review. All twelve final rollouts, including failures, are retained. The submission was accepted by the portal on October 11, 2026; organizer performance verification remains pending. This manuscript has not been submitted to arXiv.
\note{Reading the results}{Q in our result figures is official final goal progress. It is different from the learned critic's internal score. Partial progress is also different from complete task success.}

\section{Method: propose, refine, choose}
We use Ilia's released 2025 implementation~\cite{ilia} as the base policy. Its weights remain frozen. Per-task auxiliary networks follow an EXPO-inspired editing and real-time inference path~\cite{expo,rt}. We describe the executed implementation; we do not claim to reproduce every setting or result of those papers.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{method-overview.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{\textbf{Online policy overview.} The original policy proposes two action sequences. A learned residual produces one additional candidate. Twin target critics score the three candidates, and the retained Ilia wrapper postprocesses the selected sequence before execution. A four-action carry prefix conditions the next asynchronous inference.}
\end{center}

\subsection{What changes, and what stays fixed}
\par\vspace{2pt}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.25\linewidth}X@{}}\toprule
\textbf{Frozen base} & Ilia visual backbone, action expert, task embeddings, stage predictor, cross-layer key/value transforms, and correlated sampling.\\
\textbf{Learned additions} & Task-specific edit actor, image encoder, critic, target critic, and training-only auxiliary components.\\
\textbf{Retained execution} & Stage voting and correction rules. Rules can modify a selected chunk; stage voting is separate from action selection.\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\par\vspace{2pt}

\subsection{A small, explicit candidate set}
Two native 30-step chunks provide the base proposals. A single edit step with normalized scale 0.01 creates one residual alternative from the first proposal. For each candidate $a$, the online path evaluates two target critics and selects
\[
 a^*=\arg\max_{a\in\{a_1,a_2,\widetilde a_1\}}\min\bigl(\widehat Q_1(s,a),\widehat Q_2(s,a)\bigr).
\]
This is selection, not averaging. The learned scores $\widehat Q$ differ from official goal progress. The wrapper executes 15 actions and retains four carry actions. RT action compression is disabled.

\par\vspace{2pt}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lXlX@{}}\toprule
Batch size & 8 & Discount & 0.999\\
Chunk / execution & 30 / 15 steps & Carry prefix & 4 steps\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\par\vspace{2pt}
\note{Implementation boundary}{The filter critic and temperature affect training but are not directly called by the current fast online selection path. The submitted actor is the original released A0, not the separately adapted historical F1 actor.}

\section{Development pilot: no aggregate improvement}
The pilot contains six tasks, three development instances per task, and two conditions: original Ilia (A0) and Ilia with RT editing. The 36 simulated rollouts form 18 matched task-instance pairs. Development instances are native 301 (public index 0) and training instances 23 and 24; this is not an untouched held-out benchmark.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pilot-results.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{\textbf{Paired development results.} Each bar averages three rollouts. Task 001 improves partial progress, but the aggregate result worsens. Scores are final goal progress, not the maximum intermediate progress observed during an episode.}
\end{center}

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}Xrrr@{}}\toprule
\textbf{Pilot aggregate} & \textbf{Original A0} & \textbf{With RT} & \textbf{Change}\\\midrule

Task means with higher Q & --- & 1/6 & Task 001 only\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\par\vspace{2pt}

\subsection{What this experiment establishes}
This configuration does \textbf{not} support an overall improvement claim. Task 001 raises mean Q from 0.444 to 0.667 while full successes fall from 1/3 to 0/3. Task 005 has unchanged mean Q, also with fewer full successes. Increasing some satisfied goals is therefore insufficient evidence of reliable task completion.

\subsection{What it does not isolate}
The panel reuses preserved baseline rollouts where documented; it is not a prospectively randomized study. Different tasks use different actors and auxiliary parameters. The RT path also changes chunk scheduling and disables the original compression. Consequently, the A0--RT comparison measures the complete configured system, rather than an isolated residual-editing effect.

\note{Practical lesson}{A successful grasp or a higher predicted value is an intermediate event. Evaluation must check whether all required goals remain satisfied at the episode end.}

\subsection{Protocol differences matter}
The pilot used a 224-pixel RGB DefaultWrapper. The final panel uses RGBDFullResWrapper with the unchanged R1Pro robot configuration; the policy subsequently resizes RGB and does not consume depth. Public index 0 had development exposure. Different wrappers, instance sets, and runtime conditions prevent treating pilot-to-final differences as a controlled improvement or regression experiment.
\note{Recorded outcome}{Some final trials make partial progress, but none completes its full goal. We report this outcome without a significance or generalization claim.}

\subsection{A completed release, with bounded deployment evidence}
The final evaluation ran on a clean server with four L40S GPUs. Each GPU hosted a policy and simulator. Simulator CPU ceilings changed during evaluation and are documented in the execution record; they were not exclusive core reservations. Hardware and scheduling differences therefore cannot be used as a controlled speed benchmark.

The frozen Docker release embeds the weights and supports five routes: 000, 001, 002, 003, and 005. Only three routes were evaluated in the final panel. Checkpoint 1 serves 002/003/005 and checkpoint 2 serves 000/001; task 007 remains pilot-only. A policy-only L40S check passed route switching and seeded reset checks, with 7,317\,MiB peak GPU memory. Synthetic inputs verify deployment behavior, not household-task success. Actual 24\,GB hardware was not tested.

\subsection{Priorities for a subsequent study}
\textbf{First, observe the missing transitions.} Record synchronized goal predicates, stage predictions, gripper commands, and correction-rule triggers. This separates object reached from object placed and identifies where execution stalls.

\textbf{Second, isolate the interventions.} Compare A0, A0 with candidate scoring, and A0 with scoring plus editing under matched wrappers, instances, seeds, and time budgets. A separate comparison is needed for actor fine-tuning (F1).

\textbf{Third, target recovery evidence.} Use the identified insertion and stall cases to choose recovery demonstrations or bounded recovery rules. These are future experiments, not implemented features or established improvements.
\note{Conclusion}{The work delivers a reproducible challenge entry and actionable failure evidence. The tested RT configuration does not yet improve overall task completion.}

\section{BEHAVIOR data and the simulated robot}
\subsection{Benchmark, challenge, and this experiment}
BEHAVIOR-1K covers 1,000 everyday activities in realistic interactive simulation~\cite{behavior}. The 2026 challenge is a defined subset, with 100 tasks and 20,000 released demonstrations~\cite{dataset2026}. These dataset totals should not be confused with the much smaller adaptation and evaluation budgets in this report.

\par\vspace{2pt}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.27\linewidth}X@{}}\toprule
\textbf{2026 dataset} & 100 tasks; 20,000 human demonstrations. HDF5 supports exact replay; LeRobot v3 organizes learning data.\\
\textbf{This adaptation} & 20 demonstration episodes per task; 1,000 auxiliary-network updates. The released Ilia actor stays frozen.\\
\textbf{Development evaluation} & 6 tasks, 3 instances, 2 conditions: 36 rollouts.\\
\textbf{Final evaluation} & 3 tasks, 4 public instances, 1 frozen condition: 12 rollouts.\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\par\vspace{2pt}

\subsection{How a task becomes a score}
Task definitions specify object relationships and states in BDDL. For example, trash picking requires each target can to be inside the trash can. The evaluator reports the fraction of required goals satisfied at the end and a separate full-success indicator~\cite{evaluation}. Camera appearance, motion distance, and predicted critic value do not replace these task predicates.

\subsection{Dataset and evaluator observations}
The official LeRobot metadata describes 30\,Hz episodes with three RGB streams, depth streams, a 61-dimensional state, and a 23-dimensional action~\cite{datasetschema}. The final policy consumes RGB, proprioception, and task identity. Depth is available in the evaluator wrapper but unused by this policy.
\par\vspace{2pt}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.29\linewidth}X@{}}\toprule
RGB images & Head: $720\!\times\!720$; each wrist: $480\!\times\!480$ in the official schema. The policy resizes images internally.\\
Robot state & 61 floats: base velocity (3), arm positions/velocities (28), end-effector poses (14), gripper positions/velocities (8), torso positions/velocities (8).\\
Physical action & 23 floats: base (3), torso (4), left arm (7), left gripper (1), right arm (7), right gripper (1).\\
Dataset organization & \texttt{annotations/}, \texttt{data/}, \texttt{meta/}, and \texttt{videos/}; episode metadata connects the streams.\\
\bottomrule\end{tabularx}\par\vspace{2pt}

\subsection{Inside the submitted model}
The adapter reduces the 61-dimensional observation to a 23-dimensional control state: base velocity, torso positions, both arm positions, and one summed finger-position value per gripper. Nine zeros pad this vector to the native 32-dimensional model input. The native actor predicts a $30\!\times\!32$ action chunk; only 23 physical dimensions are commanded.

Native Ilia actions use their own normalization, delta conventions, and time-dependent statistics. The critic/editor uses a separate documented coordinate system. Conversion passes through physical targets, applies the appropriate anchor, and then maps to the critic frame. Selected actions are decoded back before execution. Copying a normalized vector directly across these interfaces would not preserve its physical meaning.
